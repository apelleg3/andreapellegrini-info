Snowballs, Turtles, and Bar Charts

Visual metaphor was an integrated but subtle part of my dissertation study on compound interest. Illustrations, bar charts, and visual displays from the study.

By Andrea Pellegrini · November 20, 2026

On April 3, 2026, I successfully defended my dissertation… Goal Framing & Visualizations' Influence on Self-regulated Learning of Compound Interest: An Explanatory Mixed Methods Study.

The Most Powerful Visual

Visual metaphor was an integrated but subtle part of my study. The interactive compound interest calculator which produced bar charts to illustrate savings growth or debt repayment was the most powerful visual helping people understand the impact of time on exponential growth.

Empirically-Informed Design

Large language models, Claude Sonnet 4 and 4.5, were used to draft code for the html-based calculator with interactive data visualizations in June 2025. The structure is similar to what can be found in publicly-available compound interest calculators on Investor.gov (line charts), NerdWallet (shaded area chart), or MoneyChimp (bar chart). A feature to toggle a table of the values in the bar chart was also included. The choice to use bar charts and provide the option of looking at the table was informed by prior empirical work by Hubbard et al. (2016) and Franconeri et al. (2021). Unlike most publicly available compound interest calculators, a function was added to address debt repayment vs savings goals.

Similar to other bar charts used in the intervention, the embedded visualization illustrating exponential growth uses a bar chart rather than a continuous curve to support easier comparison of yearly growth increments. In this case, to accommodate clear labeling and responsive web design for users on smaller screens, a slider was implemented to allow participants to see how money grows over time with compound interest.

In an effort to find more compelling evidence for best practices in the design of visual aids in financial education, Hubbard, Matthews, & Samek (2016) tested the use of text (table), linear graph, and "volumetric" graph visualizations on compound interest literacy. They found that "volumetric" graphs using visual representations of individual square units (which viewers could count), and "text" tables increased the accuracy of post-test scores on compound interest, much more than linear graphs which are commonly used in publicly available compound interest calculators. The authors discussed the poor performance of linear graphs as a surprise and theorized that the linear graphs requiring coordination between the X and Y axes may have made them more cognitively taxing than using tables or volumetric graphs (Hubbard et al., 2016, p. 118).

According to Franconeri et al. (2021; 2024), empirical studies of humans' abilities to process data visualizations have resulted several insights. For example, although the human visual system is extremely fast at processing information, it slows down considerably when needing to compare data points or connect visual patterns to semantic meaning. To improve visual processing, visualizations can be designed to map numbers to visual channels like position, length, area, angle, or intensity (e.g., label data points rather than relying on viewer to interpret from X- and Y-axes; use value intensity over different hues for color coding). How efficiently different visual channels can process information varies. Dot plots or scatterplots are most accurately assessed by position followed by our ability to assess length (e.g., bar graphs), area (e.g., bubble maps), angle (e.g., line graphs), and intensity (e.g., heat maps). It can be easy to misinterpret graphs through truncating axes (e.g., not starting the y-axis at 0) or skewed visual properties (e.g., 3D depictions can inflate data values; line graphs with steep slopes can distort estimations of differences between points; overlapping varying intensities, etc.).

An important but often overlooked aspect of designing visualizations that are void of perceptual misinterpretations is called the "Curse of Knowledge" highlighting that experts overestimate how well others will understand a visualization because they cannot suppress their own expertise (Franconeri et al., 2021, p. 150). Franconeri (Northwestern University, 2024) also notes that data journalists like those that create data visualizations for The New York Times, The Washington Post, The Economist, etc., leverage narrative visualization to show a single pattern at a time and directing attention towards patterns through narrative text or audio. This is the same technique employed in the Theory of Multimedia Learning to reduce cognitive load and increase efficiency of encoding memory (Cook, 2006; Mayer, 2001, 2024; Moreno & Mayer, 1999).

Icons I Illustrated by Hand

Only one participant commented in the interview that they noticed the icons I illustrated by hand and cleaned up in Adobe Capture (Adobe Inc., 2025) and Procreate (Savage Interactive, 2025). It was important to me that I drew my own graphics for the online educational intervention I created to teach compound interest.

Digital illustrations of a credit card, front end of a car, graduation cap, bungalow, and palm tree with a beach chair to visually represent financial decisions where compound interest knowledge can be applied.

Turtles, Then Snowballs

I illustrated more ideas than what I used in the final version of the short course. The little turtles were my initial visual metaphor for exponential growth, but I ended up making snowballs later since that has been used narratively to describe exponential growth.

To reinforce conceptual understanding of exponential growth, the primary differentiating feature of compound interest compared to simple interest, the educational material used a snowball metaphor. This representation visually demonstrates how accumulated interest contributes to future growth, producing accelerating expansion associated with compounding. Visual metaphors were produced to reduce cognitive load while reinforcing conceptual understanding of compound interest.

Narrative to Data-Driven

Narrative visualization is a category that leverages visual metaphors or stories. Examples include illustrations, animations, storyboards, and graphic novels (Fry et al., 2013). Alternatively, data visualizations, as the name implies, rely heavily on information and data. Software visualizations are domain specific and help communicate graphically how programs or algorithms are intended to function (Olsson et al., 2015). Typically, these are used by information technology professionals, but as our society grows more tech-savvy, these types of visualizations may be useful for explaining financial technology functions to users. Visualizations can exist along a continuum between "data-based and narrative-based visualizations" (Fry et al., 2013, p. 1233). For example, animations can be used not solely as a narrative device but also to illustrate relationships between concepts, particularly in science (Gilbert et al., 2008).

Using visualization as an assignment, educators can assess students' understanding of how to communicate domain-specific concepts (e.g., personal finance) with the use of visual metaphors. A prototype of the infoEmotion© matrix developed by Fry and colleagues (2013), provides a framework for assessing narrative visualizations for financial education. The content elements featured in the matrix include financial data, financial information, financial concept, behavioral consequence, behavioral decision process, behavioral negotiation, behavioral ethic, behavioral culture, and behavioral emotion. This tool was used to identify which elements were present in 3.5 minute clips on financial decision-making submitted by students in the "Design 4: Visualizing Finance" course at Parsons (Fry et al., 2013). In some instances, both visual metaphor and data can be combined, making it difficult to discern where a visualization occurs along the narrative to data-driven continuum.

This study builds on the concept of the narrative to data-driven continuum by including data visualizations and visual metaphors in personally meaningful ways to learners. Providing both an illustrative visual metaphor and a data visualization alongside narrative text intentionally leveraged multi-modal content to reinforce the conceptual differences between simple and compound interest.

Slow and Steady

However, the turtle still represents how I had to approach working towards my PhD as a full-time employee: slow and steady helped me finish… even if parts felt like moving slower than cold molasses then immediately at break-neck speeds near the end.

Poster & Visual Display

I included an early poster, screenshots from the course with narrative context (critical for effective dual-mode delivery), my visual display showing the phases and functions of my mixed methodology, the snowballs, and the turtle illustrations that didn't get incorporated but feel poetically relevant to my academic journey.

Process & Tools

I used pencil sketches that were inked and imported to Procreate after using Adobe Capture to scan. I used Microsoft PowerPoint to create the research poster, Word to create the Visual Display, and the images were integrated into Qualtrics with HTML to customize the User Interface.
